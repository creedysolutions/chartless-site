Nashville numbers for worship teams: how to read them, and a chart for every key

The leader calls a key change ten minutes before service. With numbers, nobody rewrites a thing.
Short answer: the Nashville number system writes chords as numbers from the key: 1 is the key's home chord, 4 and 5 are the other two majors, 6m is the relative minor. "1 5 6m 4" is the same song in G (G D Em C) or in A (A E F#m D). Change the key, keep the chart.

The seven numbers

Every major key has seven chords that belong to it. Number them by scale step: 1, 2m, 3m, 4, 5, 6m, 7°. In practice most worship songs live on four of them: 1, 4, 5 and 6m. Learn where those four sit in your two or three most common keys and you can play most of a Sunday set from numbers alone.

Reading a number chart

  • A plain number is one bar of that chord: 1 4 1 5 is four bars.
  • Underlined or boxed numbers (or numbers grouped together, like 45) split a bar, usually two beats each.
  • A slash means a bass note: 1/3 in G is G with B in the bass.
  • m, 7, sus, 2 work the same as on a chord chart: 2m7, 4sus2, 5sus.
  • A diamond means hit it and let it ring. Push marks (^) mean the chord comes an eighth early.

Writing one from a song you only have a recording of

  1. Find the key. Listen for where the song rests, usually the last chord of the chorus. That's your 1.
  2. Find the bass movement. Hum the bass note on each chord change and match it to the scale.
  3. Count bars. Write one number per bar, four bars to a line, sections labeled (V1, C, B).
  4. Check the minors. If a chord sounds sad, it's almost always the 6m or 2m.
